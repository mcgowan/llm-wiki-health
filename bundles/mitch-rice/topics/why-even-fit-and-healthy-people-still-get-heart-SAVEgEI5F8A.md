# Overview

Dr. Mitch Rice addresses the phenomenon of seemingly healthy, fit people dying of sudden heart attacks, arguing that fitness and arterial health are not the same thing[^why-even-fit-and-healthy-people-still-get-heart-SAVEgEI5F8A]. His central claim is that arterial plaque builds up inside the artery wall itself, not in the open channel, so an artery can appear to have normal blood flow on standard testing while dangerous plaque accumulates undetected, sometimes for decades, until it ruptures suddenly and forms a clot that blocks blood flow entirely[^why-even-fit-and-healthy-people-still-get-heart-SAVEgEI5F8A]. He attributes this hidden buildup to chronic inflammation, high blood sugar, oxidized LDL, smoking, and high blood pressure irritating the artery lining, prompting the body to patch the injury with cholesterol, calcium, and immune cells[^why-even-fit-and-healthy-people-still-get-heart-SAVEgEI5F8A].

# Why standard tests miss it

He argues a standard lipid panel (total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides) does not reveal whether LDL particles are small and dense (more dangerous), how inflamed the arteries are, or whether plaque has already formed[^why-even-fit-and-healthy-people-still-get-heart-SAVEgEI5F8A]. He further distinguishes "stable" plaque, which he compares to fully set glue, from "unstable" plaque — soft, weak, and prone to sudden rupture — arguing that inflammation, driven by chronic stress, poor sleep, blood sugar swings, gut imbalances, and even gum disease, is what makes plaque unstable regardless of a person's fitness level or cholesterol numbers[^why-even-fit-and-healthy-people-still-get-heart-SAVEgEI5F8A].

# Six recommended steps

He recommends patients specifically ask their doctor for tests not included in routine checkups: (1) high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) to measure arterial inflammation, targeting under 1.0; (2) an advanced lipid panel (e.g., NMR LipoProfile) to check LDL particle size; (3) a one-time Lp(a) test, since Lp(a) is largely genetically determined and not modified by diet or exercise, but sharply raises heart attack and stroke risk when elevated; (4) a coronary calcium score (a low-dose CT scan, no contrast, about 10 minutes) to directly detect calcified plaque, especially for people over 40 or with family history; (5) treating inflammation as a vital sign via sleep, stress management, reduced ultra-processed food, daily movement, and gut health; and (6) omega-3 supplementation (EPA/DHA) for triglyceride reduction and plaque stabilization[^why-even-fit-and-healthy-people-still-get-heart-SAVEgEI5F8A]. As an additional marker, he flags a resting heart rate consistently above 80 bpm as an independent, often-overlooked predictor of heart attack and early death[^why-even-fit-and-healthy-people-still-get-heart-SAVEgEI5F8A].

# Caveats

Supporting references to "multiple large studies" on resting heart rate and general statements about Lp(a) and hs-CRP are not accompanied by specific citations, sample sizes, or publication names, so they should be treated as the presenter's clinical framing rather than verified statistics.
