# Overview

Dr. Mitch Rice challenges the assumption that high blood pressure is primarily a weight problem, arguing that lean, active, normal-weight patients can still have dangerously high blood pressure because three underlying factors — heart pumping force, blood volume, and artery wall stiffness — can each be elevated by causes unrelated to body fat[^why-fit-and-healthy-people-still-have-high-blood-5DtTRRWgHAI]. He presents five specific drivers he says he sees regularly in lean patients with hypertension, each paired with tests to request and lifestyle actions to take[^why-fit-and-healthy-people-still-have-high-blood-5DtTRRWgHAI].

# The five drivers

- **Chronic stress response ("pressure creep")**: an autonomic nervous system stuck in a low-grade fight-or-flight state gradually raises blood pressure over years even in people with normal weight and labs; he suggests resonance breathing (5 seconds in, 5 seconds out) and improved sleep quality to help the nervous system downshift[^why-fit-and-healthy-people-still-have-high-blood-5DtTRRWgHAI].
- **Insulin resistance without obesity**: thin people can develop insulin resistance from refined carbs, chronic stress, poor sleep, low muscle mass, or genetics; elevated insulin promotes sodium retention, stiffer artery walls, and a more active stress response. He references "TOFI" (thin-outside-fat-inside — normal body weight but significant visceral organ fat) and recommends a fasting insulin test, tracking waist circumference, eating protein before carbohydrates, and a post-meal walk[^why-fit-and-healthy-people-still-have-high-blood-5DtTRRWgHAI].
- **Arterial stiffness from chronic inflammation**: driven by a processed-food/refined-sugar diet, low magnesium, poor sleep, and smoking history, stiffer arteries produce a higher pressure spike per heartbeat since they can't flex and absorb the force; he recommends more omega-3 intake (fatty fish or fish oil) and magnesium glycinate at night[^why-fit-and-healthy-people-still-have-high-blood-5DtTRRWgHAI].
- **Poor sleep, including undiagnosed sleep apnea**: healthy deep sleep normally drops blood pressure 10-20% overnight ("nocturnal dipping"); poor sleep prevents this recovery, leaving blood pressure elevated the next morning. He stresses that sleep apnea is not only an overweight person's condition — jaw structure, nasal shape, airway positioning, or muscle tone can cause it in thin people too — and recommends 7-8 hours of sleep and a sleep study for people who snore or wake unrefreshed[^why-fit-and-healthy-people-still-have-high-blood-5DtTRRWgHAI].
- **Genetics and family history**: primary hypertension has a strong inherited component independent of weight or lifestyle, including a genetic marker, Lp(a), linked to arterial stiffness; he says genetics raises baseline risk but does not by itself determine outcome, and recommends knowing family cardiovascular history and asking about Lp(a) testing[^why-fit-and-healthy-people-still-have-high-blood-5DtTRRWgHAI].

# Tests to request

He suggests bringing a specific list to a doctor's visit: fasting insulin, magnesium level, Lp(a) (especially with family history of early heart disease), a sleep study if snoring or unrefreshing sleep is present, and ambulatory blood pressure monitoring to capture readings throughout the day and night rather than only in the clinic[^why-fit-and-healthy-people-still-have-high-blood-5DtTRRWgHAI].

# Caveats

The video presents clinical experience and general physiological reasoning rather than citing specific named studies, sample sizes, or trial data for any of the five drivers, so the claims should be read as one physician's synthesis rather than settled, sourced statistics.
