# Overview

Dr. Mitch Rice contrasts LDL cholesterol and triglycerides as cardiovascular risk markers, arguing that in the "context most relevant" to most viewers, elevated triglycerides are the more immediately dangerous signal despite LDL receiving most clinical attention and driving most statin prescriptions[^which-cholesterol-number-is-more-dangerous-ldl-v-tCn18pLKYls]. He illustrates this with a hypothetical comparison: a patient with high LDL (150) but low triglycerides (75) and good HDL (60) versus a patient with normal LDL (95) but high triglycerides (260) and low HDL (40) — arguing the second patient, whose LDL looks fine, is actually at higher real-world risk[^which-cholesterol-number-is-more-dangerous-ldl-v-tCn18pLKYls].

# The reasoning

He argues LDL is an incomplete number because it counts total cholesterol-carrying particles without distinguishing large "buoyant" particles (relatively harmless) from small dense particles, which he says are produced under high-insulin, high-inflammation conditions and are more able to penetrate and damage artery walls[^which-cholesterol-number-is-more-dangerous-ldl-v-tCn18pLKYls]. Elevated triglycerides, by contrast, are presented as a signal of a broader metabolic problem: they indicate the liver is overwhelmed, insulin resistance is likely present, small dense LDL particles are likely being produced even when total LDL looks normal, and HDL (the "protective" cholesterol) is typically suppressed at the same time[^which-cholesterol-number-is-more-dangerous-ldl-v-tCn18pLKYls]. He cites research in the journal Circulation finding that the combination of high triglycerides and low HDL was among the strongest predictors of coronary artery disease across multiple population studies, including in patients with normal LDL[^which-cholesterol-number-is-more-dangerous-ldl-v-tCn18pLKYls].

# The triglyceride-to-HDL ratio

He recommends dividing triglycerides by HDL from a standard lab panel as a free, immediate indicator of insulin resistance and small dense LDL burden: a ratio below 2.0 is described as ideal, 2.0-3.5 as worth watching, and above 3.5 as a clear metabolic red flag regardless of LDL[^which-cholesterol-number-is-more-dangerous-ldl-v-tCn18pLKYls]. He also states that refined carbohydrates and processed sugar — not dietary fat — are the primary driver of elevated triglycerides, since the liver converts excess glucose into triglycerides via de novo lipogenesis; he cites a study in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition finding that replacing dietary fat with refined carbs raised triglycerides and worsened the small dense LDL pattern[^which-cholesterol-number-is-more-dangerous-ldl-v-tCn18pLKYls]. He gives optimal triglyceride ranges as under 100 mg/dL (lowest risk), 100-150 as acceptable but worth monitoring, and above 150-200 as concerning regardless of LDL, and suggests omega-3 fatty acids (fish or fish oil) to help lower liver triglyceride output[^which-cholesterol-number-is-more-dangerous-ldl-v-tCn18pLKYls].

# Caveats

The Circulation and American Journal of Clinical Nutrition studies are referenced by journal name only, without authors, publication year, or sample size, so their findings cannot be independently verified here; the "which is more dangerous" framing is also a simplified teaching device rather than a formal risk comparison. The video promotes an Amazon-linked fish oil supplement and the creator's paid private health community waitlist.
