# Overview

Dr. Mitch Rice argues that inaccurate home blood pressure readings are a major, underappreciated cause of both overtreatment and unnecessary worry, and walks through the equipment and technique factors that determine whether a home monitor's numbers can be trusted.[^your-home-blood-pressure-monitor-is-wrong-how-to-AVFzQbNiOkI] His central recommendation is to use an upper-arm cuff exclusively, since the upper arm is close to heart level and its larger, more easily compressed artery gives more consistent systolic and diastolic readings than wrist or finger devices, which he says are highly sensitive to hand and wrist position and can produce "wildly false numbers."[^your-home-blood-pressure-monitor-is-wrong-how-to-AVFzQbNiOkI]

# Getting an accurate reading

Proper cuff sizing matters as much as cuff type: the cuff bladder should cover about 80% of the upper arm and fit snugly enough that only about two fingers slide underneath; an undersized cuff inflates readings artificially high, while an oversized cuff deflates them artificially low.[^your-home-blood-pressure-monitor-is-wrong-how-to-AVFzQbNiOkI] Body position is the second major factor: sitting with back supported, feet flat and uncrossed, and the arm supported at heart level is required for an accurate reading, while talking, scrolling, or watching television during measurement can raise systolic readings by 5–10 points.[^your-home-blood-pressure-monitor-is-wrong-how-to-AVFzQbNiOkI] Timing is the third factor, since blood pressure naturally rises in the morning and fluctuates with stress and activity; he recommends checking about 20 minutes after waking (after using the bathroom, before food or caffeine, after five minutes of quiet sitting) and again in the evening before bed, taking two readings a minute apart and recording the lower.[^your-home-blood-pressure-monitor-is-wrong-how-to-AVFzQbNiOkI]

# Verifying the monitor itself

To check a monitor's accuracy directly, he recommends bringing it to a clinic visit, taking a home reading correctly, then having a clinician immediately take a manual reading on the same arm: agreement within 5 mmHg is rated excellent, within 10 mmHg acceptable, and a gap over 10 mmHg unreliable; he suggests repeating this comparison roughly every six months, since monitors can lose calibration accuracy within months of regular use.[^your-home-blood-pressure-monitor-is-wrong-how-to-AVFzQbNiOkI]
