# Overview

Dr. Mitch Rice contends that statins, while effective at lowering LDL cholesterol, do not address the inflammation, LDL oxidation, and endothelial (artery lining) damage that he describes as the actual drivers of plaque formation — pointing out that some patients still show plaque progression on imaging despite well-controlled LDL[^2-incredibly-common-foods-that-melt-clogged-arte-Wrr7vRM_hGA]. He frames plaque not as simple pipe-clogging buildup but as a process that begins when a damaged, inflamed artery lining traps oxidized LDL, with the most dangerous plaques being soft, "vulnerable" ones prone to sudden rupture rather than the largest ones[^2-incredibly-common-foods-that-melt-clogged-arte-Wrr7vRM_hGA]. He names two foods he says intervene directly in that process through different mechanisms: cooked tomatoes and fatty fish[^2-incredibly-common-foods-that-melt-clogged-arte-Wrr7vRM_hGA].

# The two foods

- **Cooked tomatoes**, for their lycopene content. He describes lycopene as accumulating inside artery walls and neutralizing free radicals before they can oxidize LDL, thereby reducing the inflammation that drives plaque growth. He cites a large study in which people with the highest blood lycopene levels had roughly half the cardiovascular event risk of those with the lowest, and states cooking tomatoes (sauce, paste, soup) releases substantially more lycopene than eating them raw, with absorption further improved by eating them alongside a fat such as olive oil or tallow. A few tablespoons of tomato paste daily is offered as a practical target[^2-incredibly-common-foods-that-melt-clogged-arte-Wrr7vRM_hGA].
- **Fatty fish** (salmon, sardines, mackerel, herring), for EPA and DHA omega-3s. These are described as working upstream of plaque formation by reducing inflammatory signaling, improving artery lining flexibility, lowering triglycerides, and stabilizing existing plaques against rupture — effects he contrasts with statins, which he says only limit how much LDL the liver produces. He recommends two to three servings of fatty fish weekly, or a fish oil supplement providing at least 1,000 mg combined EPA/DHA daily (up to 2,000 mg for "optimal coverage"), noting that only EPA/DHA (not the ALA found in flaxseed or walnuts) carry this benefit, and that frying fish degrades its omega-3 content[^2-incredibly-common-foods-that-melt-clogged-arte-Wrr7vRM_hGA].

# What works against these foods

He identifies three dietary categories he says actively promote plaque progression and can offset the benefits of tomatoes and fish: refined seed oils (vegetable, canola, soybean oil) for their omega-6 content and heat-oxidation tendency (explicitly excluding olive oil and coconut oil); refined carbohydrates and added sugar, for the inflammation and LDL oxidation triggered by blood sugar spikes; and processed meats (hot dogs, deli meat, sausage), for their combination of oxidized fats, nitrites, and sodium[^2-incredibly-common-foods-that-melt-clogged-arte-Wrr7vRM_hGA]. He also flags that tomatoes are a nightshade and may worsen symptoms for some people with autoimmune or gut conditions[^2-incredibly-common-foods-that-melt-clogged-arte-Wrr7vRM_hGA].

# Caveats

The lycopene study ("one large study") and various omega-3 trials are referenced only in general terms, without names, sample sizes, or publication details, so the specific "half the risk" figure cannot be independently checked from the video. The framing of statins as unable to address inflammation is the presenter's interpretation and omits statins' independently studied anti-inflammatory (pleiotropic) effects. The video includes an Amazon affiliate link for a fish oil supplement and promotes the creator's own free "blood pressure blueprint" resource.
