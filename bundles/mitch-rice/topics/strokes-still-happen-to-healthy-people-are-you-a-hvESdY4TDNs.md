# Overview

Dr. Mitch Rice addresses the recurring scenario of a patient who exercises, eats reasonably well, and has a normal-looking blood pressure at their checkup, yet still has a stroke — arguing that standard visits miss several major stroke drivers not because doctors don't care, but because routine tests weren't designed to catch them.[^strokes-still-happen-to-healthy-people-are-you-a-hvESdY4TDNs] He first distinguishes the two stroke types: ischemic strokes (a clot blocking blood flow, about 87% of strokes) and hemorrhagic strokes (a burst vessel bleeding into the brain, less common but often more severe); both can occur with little warning even in people who appear healthy.[^strokes-still-happen-to-healthy-people-are-you-a-hvESdY4TDNs] He then walks through five under-tested risk factors.

# Five hidden risk factors

- **Masked or "silent" high blood pressure.** A single in-office reading can miss both white-coat hypertension (high only in clinic) and masked hypertension (normal in clinic, high at home); sustained but unnoticed high pressure damages artery walls, promoting clot formation. He recommends home monitoring morning and evening for at least a week and asking a doctor about a 24-hour ambulatory monitor if readings are inconsistent.[^strokes-still-happen-to-healthy-people-are-you-a-hvESdY4TDNs]
- **Chronic inflammation.** Arteries can look clean on imaging while inflammation quietly roughens the vessel lining, allowing plaque and clots to form. The standard cholesterol panel doesn't capture this; he recommends asking for a high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) test, along with omega-3 intake and adequate sleep to reduce inflammation.[^strokes-still-happen-to-healthy-people-are-you-a-hvESdY4TDNs]
- **Undetected atrial fibrillation (AFib).** In AFib the heart's upper chambers quiver instead of contracting normally, allowing blood to pool and clot; a dislodged clot can travel to the brain. Because AFib can be intermittent ("paroxysmal") and asymptomatic, a standard 10-second clinic EKG can miss it entirely. He recommends a longer heart-rhythm monitor for people over 60, and notes smartwatch AFib detection can serve as a screening tool.[^strokes-still-happen-to-healthy-people-are-you-a-hvESdY4TDNs]
- **Insulin resistance and blood sugar problems.** Chronically elevated blood sugar is described as stiffening arteries, promoting plaque, increasing clotting tendency, and — via elevated insulin — causing sodium retention that raises blood pressure. He argues a fasting glucose test often misses early dysfunction, and recommends a hemoglobin A1c or, preferably, a fasting insulin test.[^strokes-still-happen-to-healthy-people-are-you-a-hvESdY4TDNs]
- **Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a).** This largely genetic cholesterol particle is not affected by diet or exercise and can be dangerously elevated even with normal LDL; it's rarely tested for routinely. He recommends a one-time Lp(a) test, especially with a family history of early heart attack or stroke.[^strokes-still-happen-to-healthy-people-are-you-a-hvESdY4TDNs]

He closes with a warning about transient ischemic attacks (TIAs, "mini strokes"), which can present as briefly as 5-10 seconds of confusion or vision change and are easy to dismiss as tiredness, but signal meaningfully elevated near-term stroke risk and warrant emergency evaluation.[^strokes-still-happen-to-healthy-people-are-you-a-hvESdY4TDNs]
