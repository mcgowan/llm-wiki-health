# Overview

Dr. Mitch Rice explains white coat hypertension: blood pressure that reads high in a doctor's office but is consistently normal when measured elsewhere, driven by the clinic environment itself triggering a sympathetic "fight or flight" response rather than reflecting a person's true cardiovascular status[^the-white-coat-trap-why-your-doctor-might-be-wro-aByqD4KLMD8]. He states that 15-30% of people diagnosed with hypertension may actually have this pattern, meaning potentially millions of people could be on medication based on an inaccurate single reading[^the-white-coat-trap-why-your-doctor-might-be-wro-aByqD4KLMD8]. He also notes the condition was historically considered benign, but says newer research suggests it may predict a higher future risk of developing sustained hypertension, so it merits attention rather than dismissal[^the-white-coat-trap-why-your-doctor-might-be-wro-aByqD4KLMD8].

His core message is that no blood pressure diagnosis or medication decision should rest on a single office reading, and he offers a practical framework — home monitoring plus in-clinic breathing techniques — for getting a more accurate picture before making treatment changes[^the-white-coat-trap-why-your-doctor-might-be-wro-aByqD4KLMD8].

# Masked hypertension: the opposite problem

Rice contrasts white coat hypertension with "masked hypertension," where readings look normal in the clinic but actually run high at home and throughout the day[^the-white-coat-trap-why-your-doctor-might-be-wro-aByqD4KLMD8]. He describes masked hypertension as more dangerous because it goes undetected by routine clinic visits, reinforcing his broader point that clinic-only measurement is insufficient in either direction[^the-white-coat-trap-why-your-doctor-might-be-wro-aByqD4KLMD8].

# How to check and what to do

- **Gold standard**: 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, worn as a device that takes automatic readings throughout the day and night; he notes most doctors don't order this routinely but says it is worth requesting if clinic readings are consistently elevated[^the-white-coat-trap-why-your-doctor-might-be-wro-aByqD4KLMD8].
- **Practical home protocol**: use a validated upper-arm cuff (not wrist), sit quietly for 5 minutes first, take two readings a minute apart and record the lower one, check morning and evening for at least 7 days, and average the results; a home average consistently below 130/80 alongside clinic readings regularly above 140/90 is described as a strong indicator of white coat hypertension[^the-white-coat-trap-why-your-doctor-might-be-wro-aByqD4KLMD8].
- **Don't stop medication** on your own if already prescribed; instead, bring a home log to appointments and ask about ambulatory monitoring or a trial period of lifestyle changes before adjusting treatment[^the-white-coat-trap-why-your-doctor-might-be-wro-aByqD4KLMD8].
- **Breathing techniques before a clinic reading**: resonance breathing (in through the nose for 5 seconds, out through the mouth for 5 seconds, for 3-5 minutes) or the physiologic sigh (a deep nasal inhale, a second quick inhale, then an 8-second mouth exhale, repeated 3-4 times), which he frames as getting closer to a true baseline rather than "gaming" the reading[^the-white-coat-trap-why-your-doctor-might-be-wro-aByqD4KLMD8].
